Introduction to Diffusion-Limited Aggregation

Diffusion-Limited Aggregation (DLA) is a process whereby particles undergoing random motion cluster together to form beautiful branching patterns. Think of it like frost forming on a window, or lightning branching across the sky.1

How It Works (The Simple Version)

The Basic Algorithm

  1. Start with a seed - Place one “stuck” particle in the center
  2. Release a walker - Start a new particle at a random position far from the cluster
  3. Random walk - The particle moves randomly (up/down/left/right)
  4. Stick on contact - When it touches an existing particle, it sticks and becomes part of the cluster
  5. Repeat - Release more walkers until you have a beautiful fractal

This is beautifully explained in The Nature of Code, Chapter 7 by Daniel Shiffman.2

Random Walk Process

The foundation of DLA is the random walk, where particles move according to:

\[\mathbf{r}(t+1) = \mathbf{r}(t) + \boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\]

where:

  • \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) is the position of the particle at time step \(t\)
  • \(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\) is a random step - pick one of: up, down, left, or right

If you want to code this yourself, Red Blob Games has an excellent interactive tutorial on random number generation for games.3

Why Do We Get Fractals?

The Screening Effect

The key insight is screening (or “shadowing”):4

  • Particles approaching from far away are more likely to hit the tips of branches
  • The inner regions are protected/screened by outer growth
  • This creates a positive feedback: tips grow faster, creating more tips

It’s like how the tallest tree in a forest catches the most sunlight - the outer branches “screen” the inner ones.

Fractal Dimension

DLA clusters have a fractal dimension of about \(D_f \approx 1.71\) in 2D. What does this mean?

\[M(R) \sim R^{D_f}\]

where:

  • \(M(R)\) = number of particles within distance \(R\) from center
  • \(D_f \approx 1.71\) = the fractal dimension

This means DLA patterns are:

  • More space-filling than a line (\(D_f = 1\))
  • Less dense than a solid disk (\(D_f = 2\))
  • Roughly 85% of the way from line to disk (1.71/2 ≈ 0.85)

For a deeper dive into measuring fractal dimension, see Paul Bourke’s excellent fractal resources.5

Patterns in Nature

DLA-like patterns appear everywhere in nature:

Lightning and Sparks

Electrical discharges follow DLA-like paths as charge seeks the path of least resistance.6

Frost and Ice Crystals

Ice forming on windows creates branching patterns similar to DLA.7

Mineral Dendrites

Manganese oxide patterns in rocks look remarkably like DLA simulations.8

Bacterial Colonies

Some bacteria grow in DLA patterns when nutrients are scarce and they must “search” for food.9

Coding Tips

Making It Fast

The naive algorithm is slow. Here are optimization tricks used in practice:10

  1. Launch from a circle - Don’t start walkers too far away
  2. Variable step sizes - Take big steps when far from cluster, small steps when close
  3. Kill distant walkers - If a particle wanders too far, restart it

Making It Pretty

To get publication-quality images:11

  • Use many particles (10,000+)
  • Color by arrival order or branch depth
  • Add slight randomness to stick positions (off-lattice DLA)
  • Try different launch patterns (line, circle, point)
